# SmartArt-Like Semantic Visual Grammar

## Purpose

Use SmartArt-like geometry to make the page structure itself explain the argument. This is not a request to reproduce PowerPoint's default SmartArt appearance. Use flat institutional shapes, the current core-reference palette, compact typography, thin connectors, and precise alignment.

SmartArt-like diagrams explain relationships. Charts preserve quantitative truth. A strong research slide often combines one semantic skeleton with one or two quantitative evidence forms.

## Selection Rule

Before designing a page, write the relationship in one sentence:

`A and B combine into C`, `A progresses through B to C`, `A contains three levels`, or `A changes under three scenarios`.

Select the diagram family from that sentence. If the sentence only says “compare exact values”, use a chart rather than SmartArt.

## Diagram Families

### 1. Hierarchy

Use for capability levels, customer tiers, organizational layers, strategic priorities, or nested markets.

Preferred forms:

- pyramid;
- stepped ladder;
- vertical level stack;
- tree with one root and several branches;
- bracketed hierarchy.

Evidence pairing:

- percentages or counts inside each level;
- representative logos beside levels;
- a comparison ruler alongside the hierarchy.

Avoid:

- equal-size boxes for clearly unequal levels;
- putting unrelated categories into a pyramid merely for visual interest.

### 2. Process and Transformation

Use for operating models, customer journeys, implementation paths, research logic, or value conversion.

Preferred forms:

- chevron chain;
- staged ribbon;
- process lane;
- staircase;
- funnel with post-funnel actions;
- before-to-after bridge.

Evidence pairing:

- conversion rates at transitions;
- duration and milestones below the path;
- bottleneck callouts at the relevant stage.

Avoid:

- arrows that do not encode direction;
- using a funnel when volume does not decrease.

### 3. Cycle and Closed Loop

Use for recurring operations, review mechanisms, customer lifecycle, reinvestment, or feedback.

Preferred forms:

- flywheel;
- four-stage circular loop;
- orbital cycle around a central objective;
- loop with an external trigger and output.

Evidence pairing:

- cycle frequency;
- KPI at each stage;
- leakage or failure points marked outside the loop.

Avoid:

- cycles for one-way processes;
- excessive circular text.

### 4. Relationship and Ecosystem

Use for stakeholder collaboration, platform models, product ecosystems, or variable transmission.

Preferred forms:

- hub and spoke;
- radial relationship;
- concentric rings;
- two-sided ecosystem;
- Venn or overlap only when intersections have precise meaning.

Evidence pairing:

- node size based on a real metric;
- directional arrows for influence;
- logos or icons at the outer nodes.

Avoid:

- decorative networks with no reading order;
- too many equal nodes around a center.

### 5. Convergence and Decomposition

Use for several drivers forming one conclusion, one target decomposed into levers, or several channels feeding one outcome.

Preferred forms:

- converging streams;
- fishbone;
- driver tree;
- waterfall bridge;
- central outcome with upstream levers.

Evidence pairing:

- contribution bars;
- weight labels;
- confidence or priority markers.

Avoid:

- convergence when the drivers are merely a list;
- visual crossings that obscure causality.

### 6. Matrix and Choice

Use for strategy selection, portfolio positioning, risk-return trade-offs, or prioritization.

Preferred forms:

- 2x2 matrix;
- decision tree;
- branching route;
- balance or trade-off axis;
- scenario-to-action map.

Evidence pairing:

- bubble size and position;
- thresholds on decision branches;
- recommended actions at route endpoints.

Avoid:

- a 2x2 without meaningful axes;
- using color alone to encode the decision.

### 7. Segmentation and Comparison

Use for customer groups, product tiers, market layers, or parallel operating models.

Preferred forms:

- three parallel lanes;
- layered bands;
- segmented arc;
- side-by-side profiles;
- nested scale.

Evidence pairing:

- counts and shares;
- representative company logos;
- common comparison axes underneath.

Avoid:

- repeating identical rectangular cards without a shared comparison device.

## Page-Level Composition Rules

- Select one primary semantic-diagram family per page. A second family may appear only as a small supporting device.
- Keep quantitative charts on explicit axes or scales even when they are visually integrated with the semantic diagram.
- Use no more than three shape depths or hierarchy levels on one page.
- Give every node a short heading and limited text capacity; move detailed synthesis to nearby editorial copy.
- Use thin connectors and flat fills from the current core reference. Avoid default SmartArt gloss, gradients, bevels, thick shadows, and 3D.
- Do not repeat the same primary family on adjacent pages. Across a deck of three or more analysis pages, use at least three different families when the content supports them.

## Design-Draft Fields

Every page design draft should now include:

- `semantic relationship sentence`;
- `primary diagram family`;
- `node and connector specification`;
- `text capacity per node`;
- `quantitative evidence paired with each node or stage`;
- `reason this family fits better than cards or a table`;
- `difference from the previous and next page`.

## Prompt Language

Describe the logical structure and its geometry explicitly. Do not merely say “use SmartArt”.

Good:

`Build a three-level customer pyramid whose width reflects customer counts. Place representative company logos on the right of each level and a retention-rate ruler beneath it.`

Weak:

`Add a beautiful SmartArt graphic.`
